How to Read a Seed Packet: Everything the Label Is Telling You

The information on a seed packet is more useful than most gardeners realize — once you know how to read it.

A seed packet contains everything you need to grow that plant successfully — if you know how to interpret it. Most gardeners glance at the picture and ignore the rest. Here's what all those numbers and terms actually mean.

Days to maturity

The most important number on the packet. "70 days" means approximately 70 days from transplant (for crops started indoors) or from direct sow (for crops seeded directly in the garden) until harvest. It's an approximation, not a guarantee — warmer conditions accelerate maturity, cooler conditions slow it.

Use days to maturity to work backward from your frost dates. A 90-day crop transplanted on June 1 matures around September 1 — plenty of time before a typical first fall frost. The same crop direct-sown on June 1 matures around September 9, accounting for germination time.

Days to germination

How long until seedlings emerge from the soil, at optimal temperature. "7–14 days" means: don't panic if nothing appears in the first week. Don't assume failure until you've passed the upper end of the range under good conditions. Germination outside the optimal temperature range takes longer than stated.

Planting depth

Depth is closely tied to seed size. Tiny seeds (carrots, lettuce, basil) go at or barely below the soil surface — ⅛ to ¼ inch. Medium seeds (tomatoes, peppers, beets) go ¼ to ½ inch deep. Large seeds (beans, peas, corn, squash) go 1–2 inches deep. Too deep and small seeds can't push through; too shallow and large seeds dry out before germinating.

Spacing

Two spacing numbers appear on most packets: spacing between seeds when planting, and spacing between rows. In intensive raised bed gardening, row spacing is less relevant — you're planting in blocks rather than rows. The "between plants" number is what matters, and it should be achieved by thinning after germination for direct-sown crops.

Don't skip thinning. Crowded plants compete for light, water, and nutrients and produce less than properly spaced plants. It feels wrong to pull healthy seedlings, but it's essential.

Sun and water requirements

"Full sun" means 6+ hours of direct sunlight. "Part sun" or "part shade" means 3–6 hours. Most vegetables need full sun; lettuce and some herbs tolerate part shade, which is actually helpful for extending their cool-weather season.

Water requirements on seed packets are general — "regular watering" means don't let soil dry out completely between waterings. Specific needs vary by your climate, soil type, and weather. Consistent moisture is more important than a specific frequency.

Hardiness zone

Annual vegetables don't use hardiness zones the way perennials do — they're grown within a single season. Zone information on vegetable seed packets usually refers to which zones can grow the crop outdoors (rather than in a greenhouse). See the grow zones guide for how to use your zone for planting decisions.

Variety name and type

The variety name tells you about flavor, color, size, and disease resistance characteristics. "Determinate" vs. "indeterminate" for tomatoes tells you whether the plant produces all at once or continuously. "Heirloom" means open-pollinated seed that breeds true — you can save seeds from the fruit. "Hybrid" (F1) means seeds from the fruit won't reliably produce the same plant.

Common questions

The seed packet says "60 days" but it took much longer — why?

Days to maturity is calculated at optimal growing conditions — typically the warm summers of wherever the variety was developed. Cool summers, cloudy conditions, or cold soil all slow maturity. In a cool northern summer, add 10–20% to stated days to maturity for a more realistic expectation.

What does "open-pollinated" mean?

Open-pollinated seeds produce plants that breed true — seeds saved from the fruit will produce plants similar to the parent. Heirloom varieties are all open-pollinated. Hybrid (F1) seeds are crosses between two parent lines and don't breed true from saved seed. Both grow fine; the difference matters only if you want to save seeds.

How long are seeds viable if I don't use them all?

It varies by crop. Onion and parsnip seeds are short-lived (1–2 years). Most vegetables last 3–5 years when stored cool and dry. Beans and peas last 3–4 years. Tomatoes and cucumbers last 4–5 years. Store unused seeds in a sealed container with a silica gel packet in the refrigerator for maximum longevity.
