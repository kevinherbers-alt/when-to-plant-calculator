Lettuce is one of the few vegetables that actually prefers cool weather — which makes it a spring and fall crop rather than a summer one in most climates. Understanding its temperature preferences is the key to getting good lettuce.

Spring planting

Lettuce can handle light frost, making it one of the first crops to go into the spring garden. Direct sow or transplant 2–4 weeks before your last frost date — even earlier in mild climates. Soil temperature of 40–65°F is ideal for germination; above 75°F, germination rate drops significantly.

For the earliest possible lettuce, start seeds indoors 4–6 weeks before last frost and transplant out as soon as soil can be worked. Row covers extend the season further in cold climates.

The bolting problem

Lettuce bolts (sends up a flower stalk and turns bitter) in hot weather — typically when daytime temperatures consistently exceed 80°F. Once it bolts, it's done. This is why spring lettuce has a limited window in warm climates — you're racing to harvest before summer heat arrives.

To extend the spring lettuce season: choose slow-bolt varieties, plant in partial shade (which reduces heat stress), and harvest leaves regularly rather than waiting for a full head.

Fall planting

Fall is actually better than spring for lettuce in many climates — the days are getting shorter and cooler rather than longer and hotter, so plants stay in the sweet spot longer. Count back from your first fall frost date: start seeds 8–10 weeks before first frost for transplants, or direct sow 6–8 weeks before first frost.

Fall lettuce often has better flavor than spring lettuce — the gradual cooling sweetens the leaves.

Cut-and-come-again harvesting

Loose-leaf varieties can be harvested repeatedly by cutting outer leaves while leaving the center to regrow. This extends the harvest period significantly compared to cutting the whole head. Always leave at least half the plant when harvesting this way.

Lettuce is the perfect succession planting crop — sow a small amount every 2–3 weeks for continuous harvest rather than a large amount all at once.

Common questions

Can I grow lettuce in summer?

In hot summer climates, summer lettuce is difficult — it bolts quickly in heat. In cooler climates or with afternoon shade, it's possible but requires careful variety selection (heat-tolerant or slow-bolt types). Most gardeners skip summer lettuce and pick it back up in fall.

How cold can lettuce tolerate?

Established lettuce plants can handle temperatures down to about 28°F with some protection. Young seedlings are more frost-sensitive. Row covers extend the season in spring and fall, allowing lettuce to survive light frosts and continue growing.

Why is my lettuce bitter?

Bitterness in lettuce is caused by heat stress and the beginning of the bolting process. Once a lettuce plant begins to bolt, the leaves become increasingly bitter. Harvest before bolting begins — watch for the center of the plant starting to elongate and grow upward, which signals imminent bolting.
