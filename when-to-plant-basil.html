Basil is the most cold-sensitive common herb — even temperatures in the 40s cause damage, and frost kills it immediately. But time it right and basil thrives in summer heat and produces abundantly all season. It also pairs well with tomatoes in the garden and in the kitchen, which is a convenient reminder: if it's warm enough for tomatoes, it's warm enough for basil.

Starting indoors

Start basil seeds indoors 4–6 weeks before your last frost date. Basil germinates readily in warm conditions — 70°F+ soil temperature, 5–10 days to germinate. It grows quickly and is ready to transplant about 4–6 weeks after germination when seedlings are 3–4 inches tall with at least two sets of true leaves.

Transplanting outside

Don't rush basil outside. Wait until after your last frost date and until nighttime temperatures are consistently above 50°F. Cold nights — even without frost — cause basil to stall, turn yellow, and sometimes develop black spots. A week of patience saves weeks of recovery time.

Basil loves heat and does particularly well near a south-facing wall or in a sheltered spot that stays warm.

Keeping basil from bolting

Once basil flowers, leaf production slows and flavor declines. Pinch off flower buds as soon as they appear — this redirects the plant's energy back into leaf production. Pinching also encourages a bushier plant with more leaves. Do this every week or two throughout the season.

Harvest regularly from the top of each stem, taking a few inches of stem with leaves. Never take more than a third of the plant at once.

Basil in containers

Basil does well in containers, which is useful because containers can be moved inside when temperatures drop in fall, extending the season. A 12-inch pot holds one large basil plant comfortably. Container basil dries out faster — check moisture daily in hot weather.

Common questions

Why does my grocery store basil always die?

Grocery store basil is typically grown in crowded conditions and sold as a short-term product, not a long-term plant. The multiple crowded seedlings compete for resources, and the plant is often stressed before you buy it. Separate the clump into individual plants when you get home and repot in a larger container — many survive and thrive with this treatment.

Can I grow basil from cuttings?

Yes — basil roots readily in water. Cut a 4-inch stem just below a leaf node, remove the lower leaves, and place in a glass of water in a sunny spot. Roots appear within 1–2 weeks. Pot up once roots are an inch long. This is an easy way to multiply a plant you like or to root grocery store basil.

How do I harvest basil for the biggest yield?

Harvest by pinching the top 2–3 inches of each stem, cutting just above a pair of leaves. This causes the plant to branch below the cut, producing two stems where there was one. Repeat this process throughout the season and you'll have an increasingly bushy, productive plant rather than a tall, sparse one.
