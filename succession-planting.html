Succession Planting: How to Have Fresh Vegetables All Season

The single most effective technique for extending your harvest — and how to apply it to every crop in the garden.

Succession planting means staggering your plantings of the same crop over several weeks rather than planting everything at once. The result: a continuous harvest through the season instead of a glut followed by nothing. It's one of the most impactful things you can do to improve your garden's productivity.

Why most gardeners don't do it

The typical approach: plant everything in spring. Lettuce all bolts in June. Beans all mature in a two-week window in July. Zucchini overwhelms you in August. Then the garden is done. Succession planting solves all three of these problems but requires thinking ahead rather than reacting.

How to calculate succession intervals

The key number is days to maturity on your seed packet. For a crop that matures in 50 days and you want harvests 2–3 weeks apart, sow a new batch every 2–3 weeks. Simple in principle; the execution requires remembering to do it.

A practical system: when you sow a new batch, set a calendar reminder 2–3 weeks out to sow the next batch. Repeat until you're close enough to first fall frost that a new planting won't mature.

Best crops for succession planting

  • Lettuce: Every 2–3 weeks from early spring through late summer (for fall harvest). The classic succession crop.
  • Radishes: Every 2 weeks. Mature in 25–30 days; succession is essential for continuous harvest.
  • Bush beans: Every 3 weeks. Each planting produces for 2–3 weeks, then declines.
  • Cucumbers: Two sowings, 3–4 weeks apart. Keeps harvest going longer than a single planting.
  • Zucchini: Two sowings, 4–6 weeks apart. Second planting replaces declining first-planting plants.
  • Spinach: Every 3 weeks in spring until heat arrives; restart in late summer for fall.
  • Cilantro: Every 2–3 weeks. Bolts quickly in heat; frequent succession keeps fresh leaves available.
  • Basil: One main sowing, possibly a second mid-season to replace aging plants.

Crops that don't need succession planting

Indeterminate tomatoes, pole beans, and peppers produce continuously until frost from a single planting — no succession needed. Perennial herbs (thyme, oregano, chives) regrow indefinitely. Large crops like pumpkins and winter squash are planted once and harvested at season end.

Fall succession planning

Succession planting isn't just for extending summer crops — it's also about transitioning to fall crops at the right time. As summer crops wind down, succession-plant cool-season crops (kale, lettuce, spinach, carrots, radishes) 8–10 weeks before first fall frost. These replace the summer garden as it fades and extend fresh harvests deep into fall.

Common questions

How many plants should each succession be?

Start small — 3–6 plants or a short row of direct-sown seeds per succession is often plenty. A common mistake is making each succession as large as the initial planting, which eventually creates the same glut problem you were trying to avoid, just spread out over more time. Smaller successions, more frequently, work better.

What if I run out of space for successive plantings?

Succession planting relies on clearing space as earlier plantings finish. When a first-succession lettuce patch bolts, pull it and sow the next succession in that spot. The garden turns over — it's not additive. Plan your space around this rotation rather than trying to fit everything simultaneously.

Can I succession plant tomatoes?

In very long seasons (zone 9+), yes. In most climates, there's only time for one tomato planting — they need 60–85 days from transplant and the season isn't long enough to make a second transplanting meaningful. Focus succession efforts on faster-maturing crops.
