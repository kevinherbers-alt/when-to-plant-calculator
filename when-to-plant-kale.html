Kale is one of the most cold-hardy vegetables in the garden. Unlike most vegetables, it actively improves after frost — cold temperatures convert starches to sugars, making the leaves noticeably sweeter. This makes kale a premier fall and winter crop and an excellent early spring choice.

Spring planting

Start kale indoors 4–6 weeks before last frost for transplants, or direct sow outdoors 3–5 weeks before last frost. Kale germinates in soil as cool as 40°F and tolerates hard freezes once established. Spring kale is good, but it becomes increasingly bitter as summer heats up — plan to harvest it before consistent temperatures above 80°F.

Fall planting — the better season

For most gardeners, fall is the premier kale season. Transplant seedlings 8–10 weeks before first fall frost, or direct sow 10–12 weeks before first frost. The plants mature as temperatures cool, which sweetens the leaves. After light frost, kale flavor peaks — many gardeners wait for the first frost specifically for this effect.

In zones 7 and warmer, kale often overwinters and provides harvests through winter and into early spring before bolting.

Cut-and-come-again harvesting

Harvest outer leaves first, leaving the central growing point intact. The plant continues producing new leaves from the center. This can extend a single plant's productive life for months. Avoid harvesting more than a third of the plant at once.

Varieties worth knowing

Curly kale (the common supermarket type) is productive and cold-hardy. Lacinato (dinosaur kale) has flatter, more tender leaves with excellent flavor, especially after frost. Red Russian kale is the most cold-hardy and has a milder flavor. All follow the same timing guidelines.

Common questions

How cold can kale survive?

Established kale plants can survive temperatures down to the low 20s°F — sometimes colder with mulching around the base. Young seedlings are more vulnerable. In zones 6 and warmer, kale routinely overwinters. In colder zones, it may die back in hard winters but often regrows from the roots in spring.

Why is my kale bitter in summer?

Heat stress triggers bitterness in kale. Once daytime temperatures consistently exceed 80°F, kale becomes increasingly bitter and eventually bolts. This is normal — kale is not a summer crop in hot climates. Plant fall kale to pick back up when temperatures cool.

What's eating holes in my kale leaves?

Cabbage worms (the larvae of white butterflies) and flea beetles are the most common kale pests. Row covers at planting prevent butterfly egg-laying. For flea beetles, diatomaceous earth around plant bases helps. Both pests are worse in warm weather and less problematic in fall.
