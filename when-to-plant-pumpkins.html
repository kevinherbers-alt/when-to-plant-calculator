Pumpkins are big plants that need a long season — most varieties need 90–120 days from planting to harvest. The timing math is simple but important: figure out when you want pumpkins (usually Halloween, late September for decorating), count backward by the days to maturity on your seed packet, and that's your planting date.

Timing for Halloween pumpkins

For Halloween pumpkins in late October, plant in late May to mid-June in most of the U.S. (earlier in short-season northern climates). Use the seed packet's days-to-maturity as your guide — a 100-day variety planted June 1 matures around September 9, leaving plenty of time before Halloween. Planted too early and pumpkins rot before Halloween; too late and they won't mature.

Direct sowing vs. starting indoors

Pumpkins are usually better direct-sown. They grow fast in warm soil and don't love root disturbance. Direct sow after last frost when soil is 65°F+. If your season is short and you need a head start, sow in biodegradable pots 2–4 weeks before transplant date — not earlier.

Space requirements

Pumpkins need far more space than most gardeners expect. Vining varieties spread 10–20 feet in all directions. Mini pumpkin varieties are more manageable. Plan your space before planting — pumpkins planted in the wrong spot will take over the whole garden.

Pollination

Like zucchini, pumpkins have separate male and female flowers. Male flowers appear first, followed by female flowers (identifiable by the tiny pumpkin at the base). Pollinators are essential. If female flowers are dropping without setting fruit, hand-pollinate by transferring pollen from a male flower to a female flower with a small brush or by touching the centers of both flowers.

Common questions

How do I know when a pumpkin is ready to harvest?

A ripe pumpkin has fully developed color for the variety, a hard, woody stem, and a hollow sound when thumped. The skin should resist a fingernail scratch. Harvest before the first hard frost — frost damages pumpkins left in the field. Cut with a few inches of stem attached; stemless pumpkins rot faster.

Can I grow pumpkins in a small garden?

With difficulty. The vines are the problem. Some gardeners train vines vertically on a very strong trellis (small and mini varieties only — large pumpkins are too heavy). Otherwise, look for "bush" or "compact" varieties that take less space, or accept that one or two pumpkin plants will dominate a significant portion of a small garden.

Why are my pumpkins rotting on the vine?

Pumpkins resting directly on wet soil can rot on the bottom. Place a piece of wood, cardboard, or straw under developing pumpkins to improve air circulation. Also ensure good drainage — pumpkins don't like waterlogged soil. Once rot starts, there's no recovery; prevention is the approach.
