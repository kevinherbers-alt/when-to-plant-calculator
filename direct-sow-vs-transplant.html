Direct Sow vs. Starting Indoors: Which Is Right for Each Vegetable?

The complete guide to deciding which vegetables to start indoors and which to sow directly in the garden.

One of the most fundamental decisions in vegetable gardening: should you start seeds indoors weeks before planting time, or sow them directly into the garden where they'll grow? The answer varies by vegetable, and getting it right saves time, money, and frustration.

Why start seeds indoors?

Indoor seed starting extends your growing season. In northern climates, tomatoes and peppers need 6–10 weeks to reach transplant size — far longer than the frost-free season allows from a direct sow. Starting indoors in February or March means transplanting robust seedlings in May rather than starting from seed in the garden in June and running out of growing days.

Indoor starting also gives seeds the controlled conditions they need: consistent warmth, moisture, and light that the outdoor environment doesn't reliably provide in early spring.

Why direct sow instead?

Many vegetables don't like having their roots disturbed, don't benefit enough from an early start to justify the effort, or germinate so quickly in warm soil that indoor starting provides no advantage. For these crops, direct sowing is not just easier — it often produces better results.

Direct sowing also eliminates the hardening-off process: the gradual acclimatization that indoor-started seedlings need before they can tolerate outdoor conditions. Plants started in the garden are already conditioned to outdoor life.

Vegetables to always start indoors

  • Tomatoes — 6–8 weeks before last frost. Need a long head start; can't mature in time from a direct spring sow in most climates.
  • Peppers — 8–10 weeks before last frost. Slowest to germinate and establish of all common vegetables.
  • Eggplant — 8–10 weeks. Similar needs to peppers.
  • Celery and celeriac — 10–12 weeks. Very slow-growing, requires a long head start.
  • Leeks — 10–12 weeks. Same as celery.

Vegetables to always direct sow

  • Carrots — Form a taproot immediately; transplanting destroys the root.
  • Radishes — Grow so fast there's no reason to start indoors.
  • Beets — Same as carrots; taproot formation means no transplanting.
  • Parsnips — Root crop; direct sow only.
  • Corn — Doesn't transplant well and grows quickly from direct sow.
  • Beans — No advantage to indoor start; doesn't transplant well.
  • Peas — Prefer cool soil; direct sow as early as possible in spring.
  • Spinach — Fast-germinating cool-season crop; direct sow.

Vegetables that work either way

  • Cucumbers — Usually better direct-sown, but can be started 2–3 weeks early in biodegradable pots if you need a head start.
  • Zucchini and squash — Same as cucumbers; short indoor window if used at all.
  • Melons — Direct sow preferred, but 2–3 weeks indoor start can help in short seasons.
  • Kale and other brassicas — Can start indoors 4–6 weeks early or direct sow; both work.
  • Lettuce — Easy both ways; direct sow is simpler.
  • Basil — Start indoors to get a head start, or direct sow after frost.

Common questions

What equipment do I need to start seeds indoors?

The essentials: seed-starting mix (not regular potting soil — it's too heavy), small cells or trays, a light source (grow lights are better than windows for most setups), and a heat mat for warm-season crops. A humidity dome helps with germination but isn't strictly necessary.

What happens if I try to transplant a direct-sow crop?

Root crops (carrots, beets, parsnips, radishes) immediately develop a taproot that's destroyed by transplanting — the resulting root is forked or deformed. Other crops like beans and corn often survive but experience significant transplant shock and lose most of the advantage of an early start.

How do I harden off seedlings before transplanting?

See the full hardening off guide — the process takes 7–14 days and gradually exposes indoor seedlings to outdoor conditions before they make the permanent move.
