Tomatoes are the most popular home garden vegetable — and one of the most timing-sensitive. Get the timing right and you'll be harvesting by midsummer. Rush them outside too early and a late frost or cold soil sets them back weeks. Start them too late indoors and you'll have leggy, root-bound transplants that struggle to recover.

Starting tomatoes indoors

Start tomato seeds 6–8 weeks before your last expected spring frost date. This gives seedlings time to develop a strong root system and a few sets of true leaves before they go outside — but not so much time that they become pot-bound and stressed waiting for the weather to cooperate.

Soil temperature is critical for germination. Tomato seeds germinate best between 70–80°F. A heat mat under your seed trays keeps the soil warm even if your house is cool. Without bottom heat in a cool room, germination can take 2–3 weeks or fail entirely.

Once seedlings emerge, they need strong light — 14–16 hours a day under grow lights, or a very sunny south-facing window. Insufficient light produces the tall, thin, weak stems ("leggy" seedlings) that struggle after transplanting.

When to transplant outside

Tomatoes go outside after your last frost date, but frost date alone isn't the full story. Soil temperature matters as much as air temperature — tomato roots don't function well in soil below 60°F, and transplanting into cold soil stalls growth even when daytime air temperatures are warm. Use a soil thermometer if you're impatient; the extra week of waiting for soil to warm is almost always worth it.

Harden off seedlings for 7–14 days before transplanting — gradually exposing them to outdoor conditions prevents transplant shock. See the hardening off guide for the step-by-step process.

Varieties and timing

Determinate tomatoes (like Roma) produce fruit over a concentrated period and finish earlier — good for short seasons and canning. Indeterminate varieties (most heirlooms, cherry tomatoes) produce continuously until frost — better for fresh eating throughout the season but need a longer growing window.

If your frost-free season is shorter than 70 days, choose early-maturing varieties (labeled "60 days" or less) or start seeds earlier than the standard 6–8 week window.

Common timing mistakes

  • Starting too early: Seeds started 10–12 weeks before last frost often produce rootbound, stressed transplants. 6–8 weeks is the window; earlier isn't better.
  • Transplanting on the frost date: The last frost date is a 50% probability date — there's still a meaningful chance of frost. Wait a week after your average last frost date for meaningful risk reduction.
  • Planting into cold soil: Air temperature above 60°F doesn't mean soil is warm. Cold soil stunts growth and yellows leaves even without frost damage.
  • Skipping hardening off: Indoor seedlings haven't experienced wind, temperature swings, or direct sun. Transplanting directly without acclimation causes wilting and leaf scorch.

Common questions

Can I plant tomatoes directly from seed outside?

In warm climates with a long frost-free season (120+ days), direct sowing tomatoes is possible. In most of the U.S. and Canada, the season is too short — you'd be starting seeds in warm soil but running out of growing days before harvest. Starting indoors is the standard approach.

What temperature kills tomato seedlings?

A light frost (28–32°F) will kill or severely damage tomato seedlings. Temperatures below 50°F overnight, even without frost, stress plants and slow growth. Protect transplants with row covers or cloches if a late frost threatens after planting.

How do I know when my tomatoes are ready to harvest?

Color is the primary indicator — tomatoes should show full color for the variety (red, orange, yellow, purple). They should yield slightly to gentle pressure. Most tomatoes can be harvested slightly underripe and will finish ripening at room temperature — useful if you need to beat an early frost.

Why are my tomato seedlings turning yellow?

Yellow seedlings usually indicate insufficient light, overwatering, or cold soil. If the seedlings are indoors, move them closer to your light source. If they're outside, check soil temperature — cold soil locks up nutrients and causes yellowing even in healthy plants.
