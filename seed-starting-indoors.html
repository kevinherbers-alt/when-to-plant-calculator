How to Start Seeds Indoors: A Complete Beginner's Guide

Setup, timing, equipment, and the most common mistakes — everything you need to start seeds successfully indoors.

Starting seeds indoors extends your growing season by weeks, gives you access to varieties not available as transplants, and costs a fraction of buying seedlings. It also requires attention to a few critical factors that beginners often overlook. Here's what you actually need to know.

When to start what

Every indoor-start crop has a recommended number of weeks before your last frost date. Start too early and you'll have overgrown, root-bound seedlings that struggle after transplanting. Start too late and you lose the advantage of starting indoors. Use the planting calculator with your last frost date to find the exact start date for each crop.

General windows:

  • Leeks, celery: 10–12 weeks before last frost
  • Peppers, eggplant: 8–10 weeks
  • Tomatoes: 6–8 weeks
  • Kale, broccoli, cabbage: 4–6 weeks
  • Lettuce, basil: 4–6 weeks
  • Cucumbers, zucchini: 2–3 weeks (only if needed)

What you need

Seed-starting mix: Not regular potting soil. Seed-starting mix is finely textured, lightweight, and drains well — regular potting soil is too heavy and compacts, impeding delicate seedling roots. Fill cells or trays with moistened seed-starting mix before planting.

Containers: Small plastic cells (72-cell or 128-cell trays) work well for most vegetables. Cell packs from last year's nursery purchases work fine. Whatever you use, it needs drainage holes — seeds and seedlings rot in waterlogged soil.

Light: A south-facing window provides some light, but it's rarely enough. Most indoor conditions are too dim for vegetable seedlings, producing the tall, thin, weak stems called "leggy" growth. Grow lights positioned 2–4 inches above seedlings, running 14–16 hours a day, produce much stronger plants than window light. LED grow lights are cheap, cool-running, and effective.

Heat mat: Soil temperature is critical for germination. A heat mat under trays maintains 70–80°F soil temperature even in a cool room. Essential for peppers; very helpful for tomatoes and most warm-season crops. Remove after germination — seedlings don't need bottom heat once sprouted.

Water: Water seed trays from below (set in a shallow tray of water and let the mix absorb it) or use a gentle misting spray. Overhead watering from a watering can disturbs seeds and small seedlings and can cause damping off (a fungal disease that kills seedlings at the soil line).

Sowing seeds

Most seeds go 2–3 per cell to account for germination failure, then thin to the strongest seedling per cell once germinated. Plant at the depth specified on the seed packet. Very fine seeds (lettuce, basil) are pressed into the surface and barely covered; larger seeds go ¼–½ inch deep.

Label everything immediately. Seedlings look identical before true leaves appear — you will not remember which flat is which after two weeks.

After germination

Once seeds sprout, remove the humidity dome if you're using one and move to full light immediately. Seedlings that germinate in dim conditions stretch toward light and become leggy quickly. Keep lights on 14–16 hours a day.

Water when the top of the mix begins to dry out — don't let it dry completely, but avoid keeping it constantly saturated. As seedlings develop, you can begin light fertilizing with a diluted liquid fertilizer once they have their first true leaves (the second set of leaves that appear, after the initial seed leaves).

Transplanting to larger pots (potting up)

If seedlings outgrow their cells before it's time to transplant outside, pot up to a larger container. Tomatoes especially benefit from being potted into 3–4 inch pots if the weather isn't ready for outdoor transplanting. Burying the stem deeper than the original root ball is fine for tomatoes — they form roots along the buried stem.

See the hardening off guide for the 7–14 day process of acclimating indoor seedlings to outdoor conditions before transplanting.

Common questions

Why are my seedlings falling over at the base?

This is damping off — a fungal disease that kills seedlings at the soil line. It's caused by too much moisture, poor air circulation, or contaminated soil or containers. Prevention: use new or sterilized seed-starting mix, don't overwater, provide good air circulation (a small fan near seedlings helps), and avoid misting. Once damping off appears, the affected seedlings can't be saved.

My seedlings are tall and thin — what went wrong?

Not enough light. Seedlings stretch toward any light source when it's insufficient, producing weak, leggy stems that don't support the plant well after transplanting. Move them closer to grow lights or to the brightest window available. Leggy seedlings can still be transplanted — bury the stem deeper than it was in the pot — but they need to be handled more carefully.

When should I thin seedlings?

Once you can see which seedlings are strongest — usually after the first true leaves appear. Snip (don't pull) the weakest seedlings at the soil line, leaving one per cell. Pulling can disturb the roots of the seedling you're keeping. In the garden for direct-sown crops, thin when seedlings are 1–2 inches tall.
